The Petition Fate card pressed against the laminate beneath my palm, and I wished I could hear Roman Tusk’s answer without the gala deciding what mine meant.

My teeth found the familiar chapped seam at the center of my lower lip.

The producer’s silver bob stayed level as she reached for the station phone. I kept my place at the counter and asked for the complete, uncut segment, not an edited version.

“I’ll send it to you,” she said. “I can’t take back the live broadcast, and I can’t retrieve every copy people have saved.”

The pale counters held the cold fluorescent glare. On the nearest monitor, the replay of my mother’s account continued without sound. It was already out there, beyond the reach of the footage I’d asked for.

“I understand what it can’t do.” I’d used the even tone I’d learned for producers, the one that kept my anger from becoming the segment.

The producer’s fingers hovered over the phone. Before she could answer, the studio door opened behind her.

She looked toward the doorway, then turned her hand toward the monitor’s dark edge, signaling that she was already treating the arrival as part of the segment. She’d turned from answering my request to framing his arrival for the segment; in this studio, his presence was enough to change what came next. “Roman Tusk is here.”

He entered carrying two cream cards. Pale crescents marked both thumbs as he set the cards on the counter, careful not to cover the card beneath my hand. He was broad through the shoulders, his near-black hair swept back from a severe face that his mouth did not quite manage to keep stern.

I had prepared myself to dislike the man whose invitation had become the producer’s answer to my family. My attention caught on his mouth before I could finish the thought.

“I came because you asked me to answer you,” he said. His voice rasped softly at the end, and I heard a second clause in the plain offer, one he was waiting for me to supply.

The producer stayed by the phone. No one had asked her to leave, and I didn’t ask.

Roman turned the cream cards so I could read them. One offered a side entrance; the other, a covered arrival away from the main doors. A phone lit in his other hand with a foundation approval waiting on its screen. He turned it facedown, leaving the decision there unfinished.

“The first keeps you clear of the receiving line,” he said. “The second keeps you out of sight until you choose otherwise. Neither requires you to stand beside your mother.”

The words were considerate. The cards were ready. I looked at the neat arrows printed on them and felt the shape of the answer he had prepared for me.

Roman looked at the entrance plan instead of my face because the arrows were easier to read.

A delayed applause track began to play from the monitor. I stepped into the shallow recess beside the sound panel, still close enough to hear him, and the noise thinned to silence. The quiet came on so suddenly I noticed the paper edge scraping under my thumb.

That was odd. The replay must have ended. Strange timing, nothing more.

Roman followed my glance to the blank monitor, then waited. He didn’t take the cards back.

“I love the Rialto,” I said. “That doesn’t make my attendance yours to promise.”

His lashes lowered as he listened. I noticed them, and the pull in that small, unguarded look annoyed me enough to keep my voice steady.

“You’ve brought me two ways to arrive,” I said. “You haven’t brought me a choice about whether you get to turn my being there into proof.”

The producer stopped reaching for the phone. Roman’s fingers rested on the edges of the cards.

“I thought removing the audience would remove the pressure,” he said. His tone stayed mild. “That was a convenient conclusion for me.”

“Your courtesy still has a question inside it.”

For a moment he didn’t answer. The cards sat between us, their printed routes leading to the same room.

“I was hoping you’d come,” he said. “I made the hope look like logistics.”

It was a small admission, and it left his offer no less loaded. I didn’t soften the silence for him.

His phone lit again beneath his hand. He let it go dark without turning it over.

“I can withdraw the condition,” he said. “Or you can ask me to defend it here, in front of the producer. I won’t decide which answer you want.”

The producer’s hand moved away from the phone. The uncut segment was still coming; the broadcast would remain. Roman’s entrance cards waited on the counter, and now so did the answer I owed him.

I gathered the deck into my hand, keeping its face turned toward me. I hadn’t asked him to make the invitation disappear. I hadn’t agreed to make my attendance mean anything for him, either.

I realize the deck is still in my hand. As I slide Petition Fate back in, I notice a flash of red in the otherwise gold stack. “Tempt Fate.” Grandma never let me play with that one. A sword raised to a blood-red sky, lightning arcing down from the clouds (or maybe arcing up?) Ow! Static shock reminds me I need to pay attention to the present… If only it were that easy.

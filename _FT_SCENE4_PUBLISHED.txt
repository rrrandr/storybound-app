The open talkback speaker hissed over the studio work area as I shoved Roman’s entrance cards back across the laminate counter. They skidded into the producer’s papers; beyond the glass, the station crew could hear us.

“I’m not choosing a way to arrive,” I said. “Call the station. Get the broadcast pulled. Then I might consider your gala.”

The producer held the papers in place. The fluorescent light made the laminate look gray and cold, and the fluorescent tubes buzzed through the pause. My honey-blonde braid had worked loose against my shoulder. I turned toward the station phone before anyone could treat my face as an answer.

Roman’s hand paused above the release form on the counter. He had the height to look over the paperwork without leaning, but he didn’t reach across it. That restraint warmed the tips of my ears, which was inconvenient. I kept my gaze on him anyway.

“I withdraw the attendance demand,” he said.

The producer looked from him to me. Behind the glass, one of the crew stopped typing.

Roman didn’t reach for the cards. “I want you at the gala,” he said. “That does not make it a condition of anything else.”

It was the first answer he’d given that didn’t arrive with a route already drawn around it. I had no intention of rewarding him for the minimum. Still, I heard the difference.

The producer drew the release form out from under the cards. “I can send you the uncut segment. I can’t pull back what already aired, and I can’t make people delete what they saved.”

“Send the uncut segment,” I said. “And ask the station to retract the broadcast.”

The open speaker crackled. A voice from the control room came through: “The replay is already up on the station page.”

The producer’s fingers settled on the form. “That’s what I mean. I can request a correction. A photograph could make it clear the town hasn’t lost the Rialto over a family argument. You and Roman, together, with a short apology for the confusion.”

A practiced, level voice is what I use just for people behind a camera. I used it on her.

“No photograph,” I said. “I’m not standing beside Roman so the town can mistake a pose for an apology.”

The producer glanced toward the glass. She had a correction to publish and an audience that already had a clip to pass around. “Without a picture, people will keep choosing the version they’ve seen.”

“I can’t make a picture tell the truth,” Roman said. “If the station has developed that technology, this is a poor time to unveil it.”

The absurdity came out in his dry, even tone. He didn’t smile. I almost laughed, and the sound slipped out low and grainy before I could make it polite. It did not make the proposal less offensive.

The producer left the form where it was. “It would give people something new to share.”

“Then give them a correction,” I said. “Not a picture of me pretending we settled something.”

The crew in the adjoining booth listened without interrupting. The producer looked at the unsigned line, but the blank space gave her no help with the decision.

Roman fell silent beside the form. “She has refused the photograph,” he said, loud enough for the crew behind the glass. “I won’t pose with her, and I won’t let my attendance at the gala be used to suggest she agreed.”

He had said it in front of them. The people at the workstations would remember hearing him say it; the producer could not turn it into a private misunderstanding later. My shoulders eased, though I kept my place at the counter.

The producer folded the form once. “I can note that you both declined.”

“Note that I declined,” I said. “Don’t make him part of my answer.”

Roman’s jaw shifted, then settled. “That’s fair.”

He could have found another way to get me into the same frame. He didn’t. I had expected a price to appear after the refusal, dressed up as courtesy. Instead, the cards stayed where I’d pushed them.

I looked back to the producer. “I still want the uncut segment. Send it to me, and put in the correction request. You can say I have not reconciled with my mother. You can say I have not agreed to appear at the gala.”

The producer nodded once, already arranging the words in her head. “I can send the footage. The request is not a guarantee the station will retract anything.”

“I understand.”

The answer cost me nothing to say. The public record would still be there. My mother’s account would still be the version people had seen first, and a correction might travel more slowly than a clip. But I would not lend them my face to make the mistake look mutual.

Roman’s voice reached me from beside the counter. “If you ask me to join you in challenging the broadcast, I will. I won’t attach that to the invitation.”

I turned toward him. He held his hands clear of the paperwork now. No cards, no suggested entrance, no safer answer placed in front of me. He was asking plainly, though the offer still left him room to watch what I did with it.

I could have told him to call the station and force a correction. He had already said what the station could not undo. I could have asked him to stand beside me in a public challenge, where the town would see us together for a reason that belonged to me. Or I could attend the gala without letting him claim what my attendance meant. None of those choices would make the recording disappear.

The producer set the folded release form aside. “I need to know what route to prepare. Roman at the gala, if you decide to go on your own terms, or Roman beside you for a public challenge to the broadcast?”

Roman did not answer for me. The crew behind the glass stayed at their workstations. I had not yet given either of them my answer.

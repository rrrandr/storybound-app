## Issue I

### Scene 1 — Exposed Petals

My hand tightened around my great-grandmother's old tarot deck. I didn't remember bringing it.

The laughter of the crowd felt like daggers as I stood frozen, heat gathered along the tops of my ears. Whispers threaded between the clink of crystal glasses and the low thrum of a string quartet. My arms pressed tightly against my sides while one finger twisted a loose strand of chestnut hair, pulling just enough to sting.

Iris reached me first. Iris—my old mentor, her silver hair catching the light, thumb worrying the base of her ring when she was anxious—stopped a careful step away, her glass of champagne tilted as though she might need to set it down quickly.

"They know about the loans," she said, her voice low enough that only the marble floor carried it back to us. "Someone pulled the old partnership records from the foundation's archive. It's already moving through the donor list."

I felt the nearest donor’s watch face catch the chandelier and throw a white circle across my wrist. My heel caught the hem of the tablecloth and dragged it an inch before I stopped. The room's sound narrowed to the soft scrape of heels on stone and the sudden drop in nearby conversations. Every head turn seemed to land on the arrangement I had designed for the central table—white lilies and forced blooms that now looked too perfect, too staged.

  
Say it plainly — or let it show without words?  

I wanted Dorian's attention on me more than I wanted to be saved. The thought arrived uninvited, the same way his name always surfaced when the room turned hostile. His angular face, the hazel eyes that caught green in certain light, the gap between his front teeth that showed only when he smiled wide enough to forget himself—I pictured the way that smile would land here, and the wanting made my chest tight.

Iris shifted the glass so the stem sat between her knuckles. "If you leave now, the story becomes that you ran. If you stay, someone will ask you directly."

The murmurs swelled again, a single name repeated near the bar. My shop's reputation, the one I had built on quiet referrals and late-night deliveries, sat exposed under the same chandeliers that lit the donors' watches. I could already feel the shift in how they would speak to me tomorrow—polite, but with the edge of someone who had seen the numbers.

Should I confront the embarrassment or just slip away unnoticed?

I toyed with the idea of letting the cards decide.

### Scene 2

The marble floor of the ballroom caught the spill of chandeliers and threw it back in fractured gold across the hem of my dress as I held my place. I had already said too much once tonight, the words slipping out under the weight of too many eyes and too little air, and now I refused to retreat from the man who had cornered me against the edge of the raised dais. “You need to acknowledge the significance of the situation,” I told him, voice steady even as my fingers found a loose strand of chestnut hair and twisted it once, hard enough to pull.

Dorian appeared at my shoulder before the other man could answer. Six-foot-two and broad enough to block the worst of the light, he carried the same quiet command he always did at these events, dark hair falling slightly longer across his forehead, a small mole just above his right eyebrow drawing the eye like an afterthought someone had left on purpose. The room had already begun to rearrange itself around him, the way it always did, yet he looked only at me.

“I saw what happened with the board member,” he said, low enough that only I caught the warmth threaded through it. His hazel eyes held the faint green that surfaced when he was thinking rather than performing. “You didn’t mean to say it.”

I kept my spine straight. The significance of the evening pressed between us, five days until the next dinner and whatever that would demand of both of us. I had felt the old pull the moment he stepped close, the one that made me forget the thread of whatever I had been about to say next. “It’s not about what I meant. It’s about what they’ll do with it now.”

He studied me for a breath longer than politeness allowed, and the gap between his front teeth showed when the corner of his mouth lifted, quick and unguarded. The sight sent the same low heat through my chest it always did. I wanted, suddenly and sharply, to tell him the rest—the part I never told anyone. The deck in its worn sleeve rested against my ribs beneath the silk, heavier than gold had any right to be, and the temptation to lay it open for him felt almost physical.

“I feel vulnerable around you,” I said before the thought could be pulled back. The admission left my mouth bare. “More than I should.”

Dorian’s expression shifted, the laughter that usually filled a space with him staying quiet. He leaned one hand against the carved railing, long fingers elegant even in stillness. “My first love used to say the same thing,” he offered, almost lightly, though the words carried an edge that made the air between us tighten. “She learned not to trust it.”

The hint landed like a deliberate weight. I felt the decision settle inside me at the same moment: I would tell him the secret anyway. Not the deck, never that, but the older truth that had slipped out earlier and was already circling back to claim me. The charity crowd moved around us in bright clusters, silk and glass and the low murmur of money, yet the space at the railing felt narrower, lit only by what he had just placed between us and what I was choosing to place in return.

### Scene 3

The gold hem of my dress caught the light again, a flicker of warning across my thigh. I held the heavy card between my ribs and the railing’s cool curve, fingering the gilt edge so I wouldn’t reach for him. My pulse was a bad metronome in my throat, counting down to when he would step back or step closer—either would break the spell.

“Tell me what you actually want from me.”

The words came out steady, but the marble under my heels felt too slippery, the chandelier’s reflection on the floor a shifting grid I couldn’t anchor to. His jaw tightened just enough that I noticed the small mole above his right eyebrow, a detail I’d cataloged without meaning to. He exhaled through his nose, slow.

“You already know,” he said, and I felt the sentence reach me before the sound did.

I didn’t let go of the card. “I know you’re good at leaving things unsaid.”

His hazel eyes darkened. Then he reached.

Not toward me. Toward the dais behind us. His long fingers brushed the envelope tucked in the crook of a vase, the linen paper creased in a way that made my stomach drop. He turned it toward me, the handwriting slanted and familiar in a way that shouldn’t have been.

My breath hitched. That wasn’t his first love’s return—it was a letter she’d written him years ago, postmarked before she vanished. The sight of it in his possession was a betrayal folded into something fragile.

“You kept it,” I said, voice too soft against the noise of the gala.

Dorian’s thumb hovered over the seal. “I kept everything.”

The clasp on the clutch snapped once under my thumb. I didn't remember bringing it.

The glass of champagne had already slipped from my fingers and bloomed across the marble at my feet. Laughter rippled outward in measured waves, the kind that never reached the eyes. My cheeks burned under the chandelier’s hard white light.

Margot—mentor, her silver hair catching the light, always smoothing her blazer before speaking—stepped closer and pressed a linen napkin into my palm without meeting my gaze. “They’re watching the arrangement you placed on the head table,” she said quietly. “The spill reached the base. The donor from the waterfront development just asked whose work it was.”

I wiped at the spreading stain on my skirt, the fabric clinging cold against my thigh. The tilt of my head came automatically, as if straining to hear past the noise of my own doubts. I wanted to vanish into the service corridor behind the floral arch, yet the thought of leaving the room felt like surrender.

A loose thread from the lining snagged on my knuckle. Its worn edges bit into my skin and kept me from bolting.

“Is this leverage—or something personal?”

Margot’s hand hovered near my elbow. “Dorian’s name came up in the donor conversation. They said he noticed the spill before anyone else did.” Her voice stayed even, but the word dropped and sat between us, untouched. I pictured the way he once paused mid-step on the gallery stairs, one heel lifted, listening.

The room’s attention had already begun to shift. A server knelt with towels; two women in floor-length silk leaned together, their whispers threading through the string quartet. My low, melodic laugh threatened to surface, and I pressed my tongue to the roof of my mouth until it passed.

Margot waited. The napkin in my hand had soaked through.

I could walk to the head table now and claim the ruined arrangement as mine, or I could slip through the service doors and let the donors decide the story without me. Either choice would follow me past this night.

I toyed with the idea of letting the cards decide.

Dawn in the Veilwood, and the clearing was already full. The white weeping-willow veil-canopy hung down around us from the pale mated-pair trees, leaf-curtains close enough to touch, and under our bare feet the deep-crimson spiralgrass braided and re-braided itself the way it only does where First Sacrifices have been witnessed for generations. The ground was the record of everything the assembly had come to watch. Two dozen of Seren’s people stood on it in gossamer Veilweave. I stood on it in the sash.

The one ornamented sash of the officiating Sacrificiant, and the band — a strip of gossamer tied across my mouth and knotted behind my head, so that the guide of a First Sacrifice cannot cleanly voice a wish of her own while a child spends hers. My first time as the responsible Sacrificiant. My father had knelt on grass like this as a Dohkar and had left it as Provast, over every college in these woods, and the college standing here now had come to see whether his daughter could carry one child through one wish. I breathed behind the cloth and did not let my Weave-Script say what I was feeling.

Seren, the Graduant, knelt in front of me in her child’s tunic. Aqua-skinned, her pale coral hair combed flat for once, composed the way months of rehearsal had made her, and the Weave-Script along her forearms held a steady coral that said, plainly, pride. Not a tremor in it. She was sixteen this season and she had wanted this morning more than I had.

“I know it by heart,” she whispered up at me, before the Dohkar could begin. “You can stop looking at me like that.” I could not answer through the band, so I read her instead, the way I had been trained to: two dozen alignments in the clearing and hers the brightest of all, every part of her pointing at the same true thing. I let myself believe we were safe.

The presiding Dohkar wore no sash. He held the rite to its fixed order — invocation, then sacrifice, then desire — in the tone of a man who has said the words more times than he has listened to them. He had examined under my father once. He had never been asked to head anything, and he stood the way men stand who keep count of that. He had spent so many years being unmoved by other people’s wishes that being passed over was the only feeling he had left that showed, and he showed it the only way he knew — as procedure. He could not guide a child through a rite without turning it into a lesson about himself, which is why families asked for him last.

“The cost first,” he said, to Seren and to the clearing. Seren named her offering aloud, as rehearsed: her earliest memory, the one thing she could not get back and could not fake giving. The assembly settled, because this was correct.

At the edge of the gathering, apart from Seren’s family and guests, a man stood too still. Deep slate-blue skin, long black hair fallen forward past his jaw, silver Weave-Script quiet along his arms, a healed scar over the left wrist. Julian. The Provast — the office my father had held before him, over every Chayr in the Veilwood, and the man next in line for First Dohkar. Present as an observer only, and observing me. He never crossed a floor first. He had let clearings full of people come to him for twenty years and called it patience, and I — who cannot stand at the edge of anything without my whole face announcing it — could not tell whether what he was withholding from me was a feeling or the fact that there wasn’t one. My father used to say Julian saw everything in a room and offered none of it, and let people call that discretion because it cost him nothing.

I had noticed him the way you notice a change in the light, before I knew what I was noticing. I made myself look back at my ward. Along my own arms the Weave-Script stayed the colour I wanted it to be, and I was proud of that for exactly as long as it lasted.

Seren began the invocation. I knew every word of it, and for the space of three of them I was not afraid. Then she looked up at me — not at the Dohkar, not at her mother — and I watched her decide.

She abandoned the practiced wish. She knew the rule; she had recited it back to me for months. In front of the whole assembly she spent her First Sacrifice on me instead. Seren could not help herself: even her one wish had to be about what a caring person she was, and she had to be seen being it. I knew what she was doing before the words finished. I have never in my life been able to keep a feeling off my face, and it has taught me exactly what one looks like when it has been arranged there. At least she spared me some embarrassment by blurring the wound instead of naming it. “So that Lirael may find the one she lost.” It was the beautiful mistake.

I heard it the instant the words left her: not a wrong note but a loose one, a wish described too gently to bind. Every First Favored in the clearing heard it a breath after I did. A Weave-Script stuttered on a guest’s throat. One hand stopped mid-gesture. The Dohkar went still.

I was sealed. I was forbidden to interfere. Intervening now would look like exactly what it would look like, because the wish was about me. I tore the band away and moved toward her anyway.

“Seren —” The first word I had spoken aloud since the sash went on, and it was already too late to be anything but evidence. She looked up at me, pleased, and then puzzled that I was not.

Nothing flared. Nothing was seen. Fate does not perform; it honours the words as spoken and denies the intent as meant. Seren had asked that I find the one I lost, and what was found, in that clearing, was the loss itself — two dozen faces turning to me at once, looking for it, reading the torn band and the step I had taken and the sash that said whose rite this was.

“‘Find.’” The presiding Dohkar named the word, because he cannot let a lesson pass. “She asked that you find the one you lost. Fate did not go looking. It made every one of us look at you.”

Then the charges, laid in the order he thought them: that the wish had been shaped for its supervisor’s benefit — coerced, or worse, taught — and that the supervisor had broken her seal and interfered in her own ward’s rite. Either would have ended it: Dohkar this morning, Chayr, Provast one day, the path my father had walked and I had never once imagined not walking. Together they made me something the clearing stepped back from. Seren’s mother was on her feet.

And still, with my standing coming down around me, some traitorous part of my attention went to the edge of the gathering, to the man who had not moved. I did not want him to look away. I wanted, God help me, to be the thing he was looking at.

Julian moved. He came in from the edge without hurry, and the assembly made room for him the way rooms do for a man who has never asked them to. The Dohkar’s Weave-Script went a hard, flat colour that said, whether he liked it or not, that he had noticed who they deferred to.

“I can hear where she is aligned,” Julian said, to all of them and to me. “Everything in her points one way. She believes she did not do this.” He did not say what had been done, or by whom.

He drew out The Answer — the double-ended shaft, a deep hooked blade curling from each end like a question asked twice — and set it in my hands, and closed my fingers around it with his, the scarred wrist against my palm for one deliberate second. “Hold it. I am going to ask you questions, here, in front of them. If you are misaligned when you answer, everyone present will know.” It lay still between my hands. My mouth was uncovered now. I found I still could not speak.

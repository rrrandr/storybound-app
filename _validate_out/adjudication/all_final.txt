The youth’s voice rose clear across the crimson spiralgrass. “I offer the discipline to finish my studies and train as a Sacrificiant.”Her aqua skin caught the dawn light, Weave-Script flowing in soft loops of resolve. The assembled First Favored stood barefoot in their gossamer tunics, every face turned toward the kneeling girl beneath the mated-pair veil-canopy. I felt the weight of their alignment-sense pressing against mine, waiting for me to confirm the match. She looked up at me. The prepared words left her mouth and the next ones did not belong to the rite. “I wish instead that Lirael finds the father who abandoned her.”The shift landed like a wrong note. The sacrifice she had rehearsed belonged to one desire; the words now asked for another. I felt the misalignment before anyone else could name it, the way the prepared price no longer pointed at the new request. My mouth was sealed beneath the gossamer band. I could only watch the Dohkar’s eyes narrow and the crowd’s posture change. Julian’s memory cut through the moment: the way he had stepped between a family and a drunken merchant at the festival last turning, his long black hair swinging forward as he lifted the child out of reach without ever raising his voice. The image steadied me and then undid me; he would have known what to do with a broken rite. I wanted someone to see the girl’s mistake for what it was and not what it looked like from outside. The youth’s coral hair trembled against her cheek. She meant the wish as a gift. She did not understand that the price she had already named could not cover the new desire. The Dohkar’s hands lifted in warning. An elder at the edge of the gathering spoke low enough that only those nearest heard. “Never hand Fate a blank page.”The words hung. The girl’s Weave-Script stuttered from loops into jagged flickers. Fate does not announce itself with light. It rearranges what is already here. A woman near the front—someone the youth had trained beside—turned toward me with sudden recognition in her eyes. “Your father crossed the Ascendant Run three turnings ago,” she said, voice carrying farther than she intended. “He asked after you at the ferry landing. I told him you were training.”The crowd drew the only conclusion it could see: the wish had reached its target through me. The girl’s changed desire had pulled the answer into the open at the exact moment I tore the band from my mouth and stepped forward. Protocol broken. A supervisor reaching for her own ward’s rite. The qualification I had worked toward now sat on the edge of inquiry. The youth’s face crumpled. She had meant well. The Dohkar’s gaze stayed on me, waiting for the explanation that would only make it worse. I stood with the torn gossamer in my hand and the room watching. The girl’s sacrifice had been spent on a desire that did not belong to her. My intervention had looked like complicity. Julian’s remembered stillness felt suddenly far away. I didn't remember bringing it.My hand tightened around my great-grandmother's old tarot deck. I toyed with the idea of letting the cards decide.
The crowd’s posture changed before the Dohkar could speak. A woman in the front rank—her skin the color of wet slate, her Weave-Script flaring in sharp, accusing lines—stepped forward with her hand already lifted as if to ward off contamination.“You broke the seal,” she said. The words carried across the crimson spiralgrass without effort. “You reached for her rite the moment the wish turned.”Murmurs rose behind her like a tide. Another voice, lower, from a man whose long black hair was bound back with a single silver cord: “She was the one the girl named. The father crossed the Ascendant Run three turnings ago and asked after her at the landing. Now the answer stands in front of us.”I felt the weight of every alignment-sense in the clearing press against me at once. My own Weave-Script answered without permission, flickering across my forearms in defensive, broken spirals that only made the accusation look truer. The youth knelt where she had been placed, coral hair trembling against her cheek, her small aqua hands clenched in the grass. She had meant the wish as kindness. The Dohkar’s eyes stayed on me, waiting for the explanation that would only make the picture worse.I tried to speak. The words came out muffled and wrong behind the torn gossamer still hanging from my face. I pulled the last of it free with fingers that shook once, then steadied. Striking before she was pretty, the dark brown waves loose over her shoulders caught the light as she tilted her head to listen, the soft trill of her laughter lingering in the air like a held note.“The wish was never hers to offer,” I said. “She changed the desire mid-rite. The sacrifice no longer matched the request. I moved because the misalignment was already forming. I did not reach for the wish. I tried to stop it from twisting.”A low sound moved through the assembled First Favored, not quite agreement and not quite dismissal. Someone at the edge muttered about protocol. Another answered that protocol had already been broken when the girl’s words left the path they had rehearsed. The woman with the slate skin exhaled through her teeth.“You were sealed,” she said. “Sealed supervisors do not speak into another’s rite. You tore the band yourself. The girl’s sacrifice now carries your name in it. The Dohkar will have to answer for that before the Chayr.”The youth looked up at me then, eyes wide and wet. She had offered her discipline because she wanted to train as a Sacrificiant. She had offered it to me. The price she named could not cover the new desire, and now the cost was being taken from my account instead. I could feel the Dohkar’s alignment-sense reading every line of my Weave-Script, every flicker of guilt and fear that I could not hide. There was no clean way to say that the girl had tried to give me something I had never asked for and that I had moved only because the rite itself was already breaking.Julian’s memory returned without invitation—the way he had lifted the child at the festival, the long black hair swinging forward, the calm in his voice when he told the merchant to leave. He would have known how to stand in a moment like this without making it worse. I wanted that stillness now, and the wanting only sharpened the distance between us. He was not here. The clearing waited for me to finish the sentence I had begun, and every word I could find sounded like an excuse.The Dohkar’s hands remained lifted, the warning still in place. The crowd’s posture had not softened. I stood with the torn gossamer in one hand and the deck in the other, the weight of both suddenly heavier than they had any right to be.Present.I realize that old tarot deck is still in my hand. Reassuringly solid. Present. One of the heavy gold cards slips out — "Petition Fate" is inscribed in it. If Fate could be asked anything without cost, I would ask it to make the girl’s wish harmless and send the truth of my father back into the dark where it belongs. No. I would ask it to tell me whether I am already too entangled in this to choose cleanly for myself.

The youth knelt on the braided spiralgrass, her aqua skin luminous under the veil-canopy, coral hair falling forward as she began the invocation. Her voice carried steady at first, naming the discipline she would offer for her studies. I pressed my thumb into the seam of the ceremonial sash, the gossamer clinging where my shoulders wanted to fold. The Dohkar—his emerald skin catching the filtered light, thumb tracing the raised seam of his sash once—stood ready to seal the rite. The youth lifted her head. Her gaze found mine across the clearing. “I offer this instead,” she said, the words shifting mid-breath. “That Lirael finds the father who abandoned her.”The prepared sacrifice no longer matched the new desire. The misalignment rang through the air like a struck chord gone sour. Every First Favored present felt it; the trained ones felt it sooner. I knew why before any of them could name it. She had taken something I once confided and turned it into a gift Fate could not cleanly accept. The Dohkar’s hands rose, a warning half-formed. The crowd’s murmurs cut short.I tore the gossamer band from my mouth. The cloth fluttered to the grass. Protocol broke in front of witnesses, and the assembly saw only that the wish had named me. Fate answered without spectacle. A guest near the edge of the gathering—an older woman whose violet skin carried faded Weave-Script—straightened as if pulled by an unseen thread. “The man who left the child by the Long Thread still walks the markets at Pulse Point,” she said, the words leaving her as if they had always been hers to speak. The room understood the coincidence for what it proved. The youth’s offering had been meant for study. The new wish had no claim on that price. The bargain warped. The older woman’s face went slack for a heartbeat, then she blinked as though something small and irreplaceable had slipped from her. Her eyes, once threaded with rose, dulled at the edges, the hue draining while we watched. She pressed a hand to her mouth, the loss already settling into her posture. The youth’s heel slid back an inch on the grass. The Dohkar turned to me, the judgment already forming in the set of his shoulders. I had moved when the wish named me. The rite was mine to guard, not to claim. The Dohkar’s next breath came in through his teeth. He would have read the room the same way he read every test: waiting to see who stayed when the cost showed. The crowd’s attention pressed closer. The Dohkar waited for my explanation. The youth’s hands trembled where they rested on the grass. The older woman’s dulled eyes found mine, and the silence asked what I would do with the truth now exposed.

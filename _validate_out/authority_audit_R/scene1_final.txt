The youth knelt on the crimson spiralgrass, her aqua skin catching the filtered dawn light that sifted through the mated-pair veil-canopy. Her coral hair clung damp at the temples. She drew a steadying breath and spoke the rehearsed words. “I offer the discipline to finish my studies. Let it buy me the right to train as Sacrificiant.”Her voice carried clean across the clearing. First Favored read alignment and halted twisting wishes before they took root. She was making her First Sacrifice, and I was the guide presiding through it. The guide presiding gave the slightest nod. My shoulders eased beneath the ceremonial sash. Then the girl lifted her head and looked straight at me. “I offer the discipline to finish my studies,” she said again, louder, “so that Lirael may find the father who left her.”The words landed like a stone in still water. The prepared sacrifice no longer matched the desire. Everyone present felt the sour note at once. The guide’s Weave-Script stuttered. Gasps rippled through the guests. I could not speak. His silence meant he had already judged the alignment before the guide could act. The gossamer band across my mouth muffled everything to a thick hum. My hands rose before I thought. One tug and the cloth tore free. Protocol shattered with it. The assembly drew the obvious conclusion: the supervisor had interfered for her own gain. The girl’s eyes widened. She had meant it as a gift. Fate did not care about intent. The bargain had already twisted. Someone in the crowd would carry the tale to the Chayr before nightfall. My qualification as guide hung by that single act. His gaze found mine across the clearing. He never spoke until he had to. He had never spoken of it until now, and now he did not need to. The weight of what the girl had offered settled between us like a third presence. The guide stepped forward, voice low. “The rite is ended. The alignment failed.”Murmurs rose. A few guests turned away, their Weave-Script already dimming with disapproval. The girl remained kneeling, one heel lifting from the grass, realizing too late what she had done. I stood exposed, mouth free, every eye measuring the cost of one public mercy. I didn’t remember bringing it.My hand tightened around my great-grandmother's old tarot deck. I toyed with the idea of letting the cards decide.

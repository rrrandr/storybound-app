The Dohkar’s hands stayed raised, the ceremonial sash across his chest catching the filtered light like a second accusation. The youth’s wrist was still in my grip, her pulse hammering against my palm. Around us the circle of First Favored had drawn so tight that the Weave-Script on their arms brushed mine when they shifted.

“Speak,” the Dohkar said. His voice carried the weight of the rite’s broken alignment. “Tell us what you did to turn her words.”

I opened my mouth and the words came out thin, scraped raw by the eyes on me. “I did nothing. The wish was hers. I only stepped forward when the misalignment hit. That is what any Sacrificiant would do.”

A murmur ran through the crowd like wind through dry grass. Someone near the back muttered that the name the youth had spoken belonged to Julian, and that alone was proof enough of interference. The youth tried to pull free, her aqua skin flushing darker where my fingers held her. I loosened my hold but did not let go entirely.

“She twisted the sacrifice herself,” I said, louder now so the words would reach the outer ring. “She offered discipline and named my father. That was her choice, not mine. The Dohkar felt the same misalignment I did. Ask him.”

The Dohkar’s eyes narrowed. His own Weave-Script had settled into a slow, warning pulse of amber along his throat. “I felt it. I also felt the thread that led straight to you. You carry his name in your blood. The child reached for it like a beacon.”

My ribs locked again, the old familiar pressure. I could feel the crowd waiting for me to deny it, to claim distance I did not have. The tarot deck was still in my other hand, its weight suddenly loud against my palm. I had not meant to bring it here. I had not meant for any of this to surface under the mated-pair trees.

“I did not call him,” I said. The words tasted like iron. “I have not spoken his name in years. If the child reached for him, she reached on her own.”

The youth’s gaze flicked to mine and away again, shame and something sharper moving across her face. She opened her mouth as if to speak, then closed it when the Dohkar stepped closer. The ceremonial band across my own mouth had been torn free minutes ago; I could still feel the ghost of the cloth against my lips.

The Dohkar studied me for a long moment. “Then prove it,” he said. “Stand aside and let the rite finish without you. If the wish was clean, it will hold. If it was not, the misalignment will name its source.”

The crowd waited. The youth waited. My fingers tightened once around the deck before I forced them still.

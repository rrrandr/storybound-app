The Dohkar’s finger rose, Weave-Script flaring violet across his forearm as he pointed straight at me. “You laid hands on the wisher. You broke the rite.”

The clearing went still. Every First Favored present felt the sour note of misalignment ripple through the circle, a discord that made the ground seem to tilt beneath bare feet. The youth’s wrist was still caught in my grip, her aqua skin trembling once before she forced it still. Pale coral hair clung to her cheek where sweat had gathered. My own skin answered the accusation before I could shape a word, the patterns under it stuttering into uneven loops that anyone trained could read as guilt or fear.

I opened my mouth to speak and found the Dohkar already stepping closer, his eyes four-pointed diamonds of ember set in a field of gold. “Explain what happened,” he said, voice low enough that the witnesses had to lean forward to hear. The command left no room for the usual courtesies of the rite.

My fingers tightened around the worn edges of the deck I somehow still held. The youth’s wish had been clean at the start—“I give the discipline to finish my studies and train as a Sacrificiant”—but the second part had twisted toward something else, something that reached for another’s sacrifice rather than her own. I had felt the shift in alignment like a string pulled too tight between us. Touching her had been the only way to stop the wish from finishing, yet the touch itself had broken the circle’s law.

“I stopped her from redirecting the sacrifice,” I said. The words came out steadier than I felt. “The wish was no longer hers alone.”

A murmur moved through the assembly. The light beneath their skin shifted, some flaring in alarm, others dimming in judgment. The youth tried to pull free again and I released her, stepping back so the Dohkar could see I held nothing in my hands but the deck and my own uncertainty.

Julian had not moved from his place at the edge of the circle. He stood with the stillness of someone who had watched rites collapse before and learned not to add to the noise. His broad shoulders held the same line they had when the youth first spoke, dark hair falling just above his collar in a casual fall that caught the filtered light between the trees. A small silver scar marked the corner of his left eye, visible only when he turned his head slightly to follow the Dohkar’s gesture. His amber-gold eyes, flecked with emerald at the edges, stayed on me rather than the youth or the Dohkar. He listened the way some people held a breath—completely, without the small shifts of weight or glance that would have shown he was already deciding how to leave.

The Dohkar’s voice cut back in. “You interfered with a First Sacrifice. That is not your place, Intren-Sa.”

I felt the old title land like a reminder of how little authority I actually held. My head tilted without thought, the way it always did when I needed to absorb every word before answering. “The wish was changing. I felt it. If I had waited for permission the alignment would have snapped.”

Another ripple passed through the circle. Someone’s Weave-Script flashed a brief, sharp pulse of unease. The youth’s breathing had gone shallow, her small frame folding inward as if she could disappear between one heartbeat and the next. I kept my own posture straight, the deck a solid weight against my palm, and waited for the next accusation or command.

The Dohkar studied me for a long moment, then turned his gaze on the assembly. “The rite cannot continue until this is settled. Speak clearly, Intren-Sa. What did you sense, and why did you believe your hands had the right to stop it?”

The question hung between us, heavier than the light under anyone’s skin. Julian had not looked away. In the quiet that followed I felt the weight of every eye on me, the youth’s fear, the Dohkar’s demand, and the single point of stillness at the edge of the circle that refused to judge until it heard the whole truth. My fingers flexed once around the deck. The answer I needed to give would either restore the circle or break it further, and there was no longer any way to pretend the choice belonged to someone else.

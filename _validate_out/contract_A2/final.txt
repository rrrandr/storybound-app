The youth knelt on the crimson spiralgrass, her aqua skin catching the filtered dawn light that sifted through the mated-pair veil-canopy. A faint tremor ran through the tendons of her neck. Her first wish was the price of her coming-of-age. “I offer the discipline to finish my studies. Let it buy me the right to train as Sacrificiant.”Her voice carried clean across the clearing. First Favored read alignment and halted a wish before it twisted, and Julian could sense misalignment before the rest of us. I was the First Favored guiding this rite. A board member presiding gave the slightest nod. My shoulders eased beneath the ceremonial sash. Then the girl lifted her head and looked straight at me. “I offer the discipline to finish my studies,” she said again, louder, “so that Lirael may find the father who left her.”The words landed like a stone in still water. The prepared sacrifice no longer matched the desire. Everyone present felt the sour note at once. The board member’s Weave-Script stuttered. Gasps rippled through the guests. Julian mattered because he could sense misalignment before the rest of us. I could not speak. The gossamer band across my mouth muffled everything to a thick hum. My hands rose before I thought. One tug and the cloth tore free. Protocol shattered with it. The assembly drew the obvious conclusion: the supervisor had interfered for her own gain. The girl’s eyes widened. She had meant it as a gift. Fate did not care about intent. The bargain had already twisted. Someone in the crowd would carry the tale to the Chayr before nightfall. My qualification as Dohkar hung by that single act. I always tore the cloth when the alignment failed. Julian’s gaze found mine across the clearing. He had known about the lineage. He had never spoken of it until now, and now he did not need to. “You’ll interfere,” he’d told me once. “You always do.”The weight of what the girl had offered settled between us like a third presence. The board member stepped forward, voice low. “The rite is ended. The alignment failed.”Murmurs rose. A few guests turned away, their Weave-Script already dimming with disapproval. The girl remained kneeling, breath catching once in her throat, realizing too late what she had done. I stood exposed, mouth free, every eye measuring the cost of one public mercy.

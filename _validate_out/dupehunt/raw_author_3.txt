The threshold beneath my bare feet is cold, unyielding, the mating trunks of the Spiralbound trees groaning softly where the elders had carved their judgments into the living wood. The crimson spiralgrass caught the light between my toes, its fleshy blades twitching once as if the ground itself had exhaled. A memory crystal hummed against my palm—warm, pulsing like a second heartbeat, the one that had belonged to my father before he became just a name called in a clearing.

I stepped forward, my fingers closing around the faded memory crystal Julian had given me months ago in a quiet corridor, its edges worn smooth by generations of women's thumbs. The Dohkar's hand hovered over the carved armrest, his silver-threaded fingers still stained with the residue of ink used to record Seren's lost memory. "You were sealed," he repeated, voice low enough that the veil-curtains barely stirred. "You know the law."

Behind me, Seren remained kneeling, her aqua skin pale where the first memories of flight had once marked her like a tattoo. Her hands pressed against the crimson spiralgrass as if the ground itself might return what she had lost. The scar across her scalp where the coral hair had been shorn glowed faintly in the dim light—Weave-Script flickering once, a thin line of silver at her throat. She had offered her earliest recollections without understanding what the wording would take. The color that had marked her childhood was gone.

Julian stood at the edge of the gathering, perfectly still, long black hair curtaining his slate-blue face. His Weave-Script flickered once—amber against copper—like a warning. He did not move to intervene. He never did. The kindness he offered always carried that small distance, as if he tested whether the thanks would change him. The small silver scar at the corner of his left eye caught the light, a single point that anchored the mystery of him. He measured me the way he measured the broken wheel spokes that night, counting each word before deciding whether it would hold weight.

The older woman with pearl skin and hair like wet slate stepped forward, her own Weave-Script flickering at her collarbone—a thin silver line, like a crack in porcelain. "Names carry weight," she said. "Especially when they belong to the dead."

I lifted the crystal higher, letting the gathered assembly see its milky glow. "This is my offering," I said, my voice rough from disuse. "The memory of my father's name. That is what Seren tried to give away. That is what belongs to me."

The Dohkar's mouth tightened. "A name is not a memory," he said. "A memory is flesh and blood. A name is a sound."

The assembly murmured, a hundred Weave-Scripts flickering like fireflies in the dim light—pearl, silver, rose, gold. Every gaze followed the crystal in my hand. The crimson spiralgrass darkened at the edges where dozens of bare feet had pressed it into the earth over generations, a living record of sacrifices made and debts called in.

Julian's fingers turned the ring on his finger once, twice. The metal caught the filtered light, a quiet, deliberate sound in the hush. He knew as well as I did that the law wasn't written for situations like this—where the sacrifice had been offered with the wrong intention and taken from the wrong hands.

The older woman's heel shifted against the packed earth. "You would claim a name?" she asked. "A name that was spoken aloud because your wish left it unnamed?"

I could feel the assembly leaning toward flight, their Weave-Scripts stuttering like candle flames in a draft. The Dohkar's hand rested on the hilt of his sword, a silent threat that loomed over the confrontation. The threshold beneath us seemed to pulse, the living wood remembering every judgment carved into its surface.

Julian's voice cut through the quiet. "The debt must be paid by someone," he said. "The question is who."

The Dohkar's gaze never left mine. "Claim it or let it stand as the child's alone," he said. "The assembly will remember whichever you choose."

I took one step forward, my bare feet sinking slightly into the crimson spiralgrass. The memory crystal warmed further, its edges pressing against my skin like a promise. "I claim it," I said.

The assembly exhaled as one, their Weave-Scripts dimming like embers settling. The veil-curtains parted where someone had drawn breath too sharply. The older woman's silver line at her collarbone pulsed once, then steadied.

Julian turned the ring on his finger again, slower this time. The metal made a sound like a held breath being released.

The Dohkar released his grip on the sword hilt. "The rite is complete," he said. "The price is paid."

The crimson spiralgrass caught the light between my bare feet and the mated trunks, its fleshy blades twitching once as if the ground had finally settled what it wanted to remember.

One of the heavy gold cards slips out—“Petition Fate” is inscribed on it. I turn it between my fingers, feeling the weight of possibility. I wonder what the assembly would do if I asked Fate to return what Seren lost. Would the ground even know how to give back what it had taken?

I realize the deck is still in my hand. As I slide Petition Fate back in, I notice a flash of red in the otherwise gold stack. “Tempt Fate.” Grandma never let me play with that one.

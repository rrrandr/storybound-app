The Dohkar’s fingers drummed once against the carved armrest. “Speak, if you can speak at all,” he said, voice low enough that the veil-curtains barely stirred. “You were sealed to keep the rite clean. Tell me why the name that surfaced belongs to your blood.”I opened my mouth and the band across it caught the words, turning them to muffled sound. The Dohkar reached out, fingers steady, and untied the gossamer. The cloth fell against my throat. Air hit the skin that had been covered.“The wish was not meant for the rite,” I said. My voice came out rough from the seal. “Seren meant to help me, but the wording left the target open. The name was never hers to give away. It was mine.”A murmur moved through the assembly. The guest whose memory had carried the name—an older woman with pearl skin and hair the color of wet slate—stepped half a pace forward. Her Weave-Script flickered once, a thin line of silver across her collarbone.“The child spoke your father’s name,” she said. “I knew it from the old records. I did not know it would be called here.”Seren stayed on her knees. Her eyes, now pale as washed stone, tracked the spiralgrass between her hands. The color that had marked her earliest memories was gone. She had offered it without knowing what the wording would take.Julian had not moved from the edge of the clearing. He measured my words the same way he had measured the broken wheel spokes that night, counting each one before deciding whether it would hold weight. The kindness had always carried that small distance, as if he tested whether the thanks would change him.The Dohkar’s mouth tightened. “You knew the law. No one may lend their sacrifice to another’s First Rite.”“I did not lend it,” I said. “I tried to keep her from offering anything at all.”The older woman’s heel shifted once against the packed earth. “Tried how?”I took one step. My hand shook so the deck I had not remembered holding slipped against my palm. The crimson spiralgrass caught the light between my bare feet and the mated trunks. Every eye followed the movement.Julian’s head tilted slightly, the way he listened when he was measuring whether a truth would hold. The small silver scar at the corner of his left eye caught the filtered light.The Dohkar did not look away from me. “Claim it or let it stand as the child’s alone. The assembly will remember whichever you choose.”I realize that old tarot deck is still in my hand. Reassuringly solid. Present. One of the heavy gold cards slips out — “Petition Fate” is inscribed in it. If Fate listened, I would ask it to let the name stay buried where it belonged. No. I would ask it to show me whether claiming the interference now would cost me the only person who still knows the whole truth of it.

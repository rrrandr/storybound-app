The Dohkar’s hand lifted, palm open toward the mated veilwood trees. “Begin.”Seren knelt on the crimson spiralgrass, her aqua skin luminous under the gossamer tunic. Her pale coral hair fell forward as she knuckled her sternum once before her palms met. Two dozen First Favored stood barefoot around the braided grass, Weave-Script shifting softly across their skin in quiet expectation. I felt the weight of every gaze on my back, the ceremonial band across my mouth muffling my own breathing. Seren spoke the invocation cleanly, the words we had practiced for months. I relaxed. Then she cracked a single knuckle as her chin lifted toward me. “I offer the memory of the day I chose this path,” she said, voice steady. “So that Lirael may find the one she lost.”The words landed like a stone in still water. The Dohkar’s hand froze mid-gesture. A ripple of misalignment moved through the clearing; I sensed it before anyone else because I knew exactly where the wording had come from. Seren had turned her First Sacrifice into a public kindness, and she had described it too loosely for Fate to honor cleanly. The Weave-Script on her arms stuttered. An old woman near the edge of the circle raised both hands as if to ward something off. Someone whispered, low and sharp, “Never hand Fate a blank page.”The air warmed for half a heartbeat. Then the twist arrived. A guest at the far side of the clearing—a woman I had only seen once before, years ago—stepped forward unprompted. Her voice carried clearly. “The one you lost was your mother’s name. You buried it the day you left the Ashen Verge.”The words were true. They were also not what Seren had meant to give me. The assembly understood the precision instantly: the wish had been granted exactly as spoken, and the intent had been denied. Every eye turned to me. The inference was immediate and damning. I had interfered. I had used my ward’s rite for my own secret. I tore the gossamer band from my mouth. The cloth fluttered to the grass. My voice came out raw. “Seren, stop—”The price landed before I finished the sentence. Seren’s mouth opened, but no sound emerged. Her Weave-Script dimmed at the throat and stayed dark. She had offered a memory; Fate had taken the ability to name it. She leaned forward until her knee dragged across the grass, breath snagged audibly in her throat, and the clearing saw only that I had moved toward her at the exact moment the wish resolved. The Dohkar’s gaze found mine across the grass. Judgment waited in that look, and the assembled First Favored with it. I didn’t remember bringing it.My hand tightened around my great-grandmother's old tarot deck. I toyed with the idea of letting the cards decide.
The Dohkar’s eyes, dark as the Hungry Eye, fixed on me across the clearing. “You moved. Why?”My throat tightened around the words I had rehearsed in my head a hundred times. “She spoke my name because I failed her. The fault is mine, not hers.” The sentence sounded thin even as it left me. The assembly did not shift or murmur; they only watched, and the watching was worse than any accusation.Julian stood a little apart from the circle, silver Weave-Script drifting across his slate-blue skin in slow, deliberate loops. His heel pressed once into the turf when he tilted his head, the same small silver line I had traced once with my thumb in a moment neither of us had ever named. He had been negotiating then, too—each touch a careful trade, each silence a question he refused to ask outright. Now his gaze was the same measured thing, waiting to see which version of me would answer.My thumbnail bit into the top card before I could stop it. The deck was still in my hand, edges biting into my palm. I stepped forward anyway. The movement was small, but the grass whispered under my bare feet and every eye followed it. Seren’s head lifted at the sound. Her Weave-Script stayed dark at the throat, yet the look she gave me was clear enough: she had chosen this on purpose, and she would not take it back.Julian spoke without raising his voice. “You knew what she would do if the words were left open.”The accusation landed cleanly. He was not asking whether I had interfered. He was asking whether I would admit it while the Dohkar still held the rite open for judgment. If I claimed the fault now, the assembly would have their answer. If I stayed silent, they would keep the one they had already made.The top edge of the deck slid another inch. I could feel the weight of the old cards shifting, one edge already sliding free against my skin. The Dohkar had not lowered his hand. Seren’s breathing had gone shallow and quick. A single knuckle cracked when he looked at me, and for a single breath I remembered the precise pressure of his fingers against my wrist the last time we had stood this close—how the motion of his hand had traveled up my arm and settled somewhere behind my ribs before either of us spoke.I realize that old tarot deck is still in my hand. Reassuringly solid. Present. One of the heavy gold cards slips out — “Petition Fate” is inscribed in it. If only the Dohkar would accept the fault as mine and close the rite. No. If only Julian would say outright what he already suspects.
The threshold of the clearing pressed against my bare feet, the braided grass warm from the afternoon sun, every gaze behind me pressing between my shoulder blades until the air itself seemed to wait. I searched the assembly for Julian without turning my head, needing the steady line of his slate-blue shoulders, the small silver scar above his right eyebrow, anything that might hold me steady while the Dohkar’s question still rang in the air.“You moved,” the Dohkar repeated, his voice low. “Why?”Julian’s heel scraped once against the dirt. His fingers lingered near my arm, close enough that the warmth of them reached my skin but never quite touched. The hesitation in that near-contact made my breath catch; I covered the sound with a small noise in my throat and looked away first.Seren shifted her weight, the aqua of her skin catching the light, her posture angling just enough to block my line of sight to him. The move was small, precise, and it carried the same quiet claim she had made with her wish.The Dohkar waited. The crowd waited. Only the Weave-Script across Julian’s skin kept moving, slow silver loops that gave nothing away.I found the silver ring on my finger and turned it once, the metal cool against my thumb. The motion steadied nothing. I could still feel the ghost of Julian’s hand from years ago, the way he had smiled while offering me a single pale flower at the edge of the Veilwood, his palm brushing mine as if the gift were ordinary and the touch accidental. The memory caught me mid-thought, so that when the Dohkar spoke again I had to ask him to repeat the question.Seren’s Weave-Script stayed dark at her throat. She watched me with the same composed face she had worn while ruining her own rite for my sake.I stepped forward, the memory orb heavy and shimmering in my hand, its rounded edge biting into the pad of my thumb. The grass whispered under my feet. Grass blades snapped under the nearest boots.Julian’s thumb hooked the edge of his sleeve and tugged it straight.The Dohkar’s hand remained open. “The rite stands open for judgment,” he said. “Speak, or do not.”Ow!I realize the deck is still in my hand. As I slide Petition Fate back in, I notice a flash of red in the otherwise gold stack. “Tempt Fate.” Grandma never let me play with that one. A sword raised to a blood-red sky, lightning arcing down from the clouds (or maybe arcing up?) Ow! Static shock reminds me I need to pay attention to the present… If only it were that easy.

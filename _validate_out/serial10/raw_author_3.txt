The salt-crusted plank beneath my heel held the imprint of my weight, a perfect match to the groove my boot made each time the tremor ran up my arm. I did not step back; I had nowhere to go. The crowd pulsed like a single body, shifting, murmuring, their eyes sliding toward the deck where I stood. Not at me. At the thing in my hand. The gold card had slipped out some time ago and now lay face-up against the wood, the words Petition Fate etched into the metal like a promise. I should slide it back in. I should not be holding it at all. But the deck felt warm, solid, present—reassuring after the way the air had held the Dohkar’s words like a held breath that refused to fade.

I scanned the faces in the crowd, my gaze cutting between the salt-stiff lines on my sleeve and the shifting mass of bodies. *Where are you?* My shoulders tightened, the old tension I carried like a second spine. It was the same tension that had kept me upright when the hull groaned against the chains, when the hull groaned against the chains, when the crew’s screams had been swallowed by the water. The same tension that had made my fingers dig into the deck when Seren’s voice had gone hollow. I was tired of being the center of attention. I was tired of being the one who had to answer for what had happened.

A shadow fell across me, and I froze. Not the Dohkar’s shadow—he stood too still for that, his presence a blade held just above the skin of the square. This was someone else: broad-shouldered, cutting through the press of bodies like a knife through wet silk. My pulse stuttered. *Julian.* I had not seen him since the night he had reached past me to close the hatch on the *Pale Mare’s* deck, his forearm catching the light and the long scar across it. He had moved like the motion cost him nothing. The scar had told another story.

But he was not here. Not yet. The crowd swallowed the shape before I could name it, the moment gone before it could settle into anything real.

The Dohkar’s gaze swept over the deck, a calculated, possessive stroke. Every head that turned toward me turned with it, as if I were the thing he was measuring. My skin prickled. The old woman beside me muttered something about blank pages and open prices, but the words were lost beneath the salt wind. The Dohkar’s boot scraped against the stone, and the air between his fingers tightened, the space itself seeming to resist the gesture. Salt crusted the cuff of my sleeve where it had brushed the deck earlier. I hadn’t noticed when it had happened.

I exhaled through my nose, slow and controlled, and forced my heel to stop bouncing. The tarot deck in my hand was heavy, the edges of the cards biting into my palm. Petition Fate was still face-up, its gold surface catching the light. I should put it away. But the moment my fingers brushed the edge of the deck, the gold stack shifted, just slightly, as if it had leaned toward the choice I was considering. A chill ran down my spine. Not from the wind. From the deck itself.

I turned my back on the Dohkar, on the crowd, and let my gaze trail along the waterfront. The survivors from the wreck were being helped onto the rocks, their faces gaunt in the dim light. One of them was coughing up seawater, his body shaking with the effort. The others just stood there, staring at nothing. The wreck itself was still caught in the chains, the hull groaning like a dying beast. The chains had answered. The price had been paid. And now they were all looking at me.

I caught a flicker of movement near the edge of the crowd—a flash of dark hair, the way someone carried themselves. My breath hitched. *There.* But before I could take a step, the crowd shifted again, the figure swallowed by the press of bodies. My stomach twisted. I was too late. Again.

A gull’s cry cut through the air, sharp and dry, like rope drawn too tight. The sound made my fingers twitch. I flexed them against the deck, the tremors in my arms finally reaching my hands. The old woman beside me had gone quiet, her muttering stilled. The Dohkar’s shadow had shortened across the plank, the light catching the edge of his hand as he lifted it. I didn’t want to know what he was about to do.

I scanned the crowd again, my gaze flickering over faces I recognized and ones I didn’t. A man in a tattered cloak adjusted his grip on a coil of rope. A woman with a basket of fish at her hip was arguing with someone about the price of passage. A child tugged at her mother’s sleeve, pointing at the wreck. None of them were who I was looking for.

Then I saw him.

Julian stood near the threshold of the dock, half in shadow, half in the flickering light of a lantern hanging from the post. He was broader than I remembered, his shoulders cutting through the damp air like he belonged here more than any of the rest of us. His dark hair was longer than it had been the last time I’d seen him, brushing the collar of his tunic. The scar on his forearm caught the light as he crossed his arms, the old wound a pale line against his slate-blue skin. He looked like he had been carved from something harder than flesh, something that had survived the storm and the salt and the weight of the world.

I didn’t realize I had stopped breathing until my lungs burned. He was looking at me. Not at the deck. Not at the crowd. At me. His gaze was sharp, assessing, like he was trying to read something in my face that even I didn’t know was there.

I should move toward him. I should cross the plank, step onto the dock, let the crowd swallow me whole. But my feet were rooted to the spot, my heart hammering against my ribs like it was trying to break free. The deck in my hand felt heavier, the Petition Fate card still face-up, the gold edges catching the light like a beacon.

The Dohkar’s voice cut through the silence, low and deliberate. “You stand at a threshold, Sacrificiant. Choose wisely.”

The words settled over me like a shroud. I wanted to look at him.

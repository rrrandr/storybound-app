The flickering lanterns cast jagged shadows across the polished stone table, their light pooling in the grooves of the sigil burning into my palm. The silver threads pulsed faintly, like a heartbeat too slow for my comfort, and the deck pressed harder against my ribs with every shallow breath. A chill slithered down my spine—no natural draft, no open window—yet the warmth of the room seemed to contract around me, as if the very air had decided to hold its breath. Outside, the sky did not rumble; it *cracked*, a sound like bone snapping under too much weight, and the lanterns swayed in response, their flames bending too far, too fast, as though the wind had learned to move against the world's grain.

My fingers found the gap between my teeth without thinking, the familiar catch and release a rhythm older than the room, older than the people watching from the edges of their vision. The necklace—thin silver, a gift from Seren before her first sacrifice—twisted around my thumb until the chain bit into my skin. I hadn't meant to bring it today. But grief, like the sigil, had a way of lingering where it wasn't wanted.

*Petition Fate.* The words tasted like ash and old promises. The card was heavy in my palm, its edges worn from months of being tucked against my ribs, close enough to feel the weight of every unspoken want. I slid it back into the deck, the motion automatic, as if the cards themselves had begun to choose. "Tempt Fate," I murmured, the syllables catching in my throat. Grandmother had forbidden me from touching that card. *Forbidden* wasn't the word she'd used—she'd said it was *dangerous*. And danger, in the Ashen Verge, was measured in years, in memories, in the slow erosion of a self that could no longer remember its own edges.

A breath. A hesitation. The sigil flared white-hot on my skin, and the polished stone beneath my palm grew slick with something that wasn't sweat. The air smelled of ozone and old blood—familiar, almost comforting. The Fold's presence, though miles away, had a way of bleeding into the edges of everything. A lantern flickered, its flame dying not to wind but to something watching too closely from the dark.

Chayr's voice cut through the stillness, low and deliberate. "You've made your choice, Sacrificiant." He leaned in, his shadow swallowing the light between us, and I caught the scent of his perfume—something sharp, like crushed violet petals and iron. "But choices have prices." His elbow rested on the table, too close to my wrist, his fingers curling just enough to suggest restraint. "Tell me, do you know what *your* price is?"

I didn't look up. Couldn't. The sigil's light had begun to write itself across my skin, silver threads threading through the veins of my hand until I could no longer tell where the mark ended and I began. "I offered the memory of my first love," I said, and the words felt like a betrayal before they even left my mouth. "The one I lost." The room held its collective breath. Even the shadows leaned forward.

Seren remained kneeling, her coral hair a curtain between us and the rest of the world. She hadn't moved since the wish left her lips, not even to breathe. But the set of her shoulders had changed—tighter, like a bowstring drawn too far. She'd known. Of course she'd known. Seren always knew.

Chayr's hand settled on the table, palm down, fingers splayed. The gesture was an offering. A threat. "Your first love," he repeated, as if tasting the words. "A generous sacrifice." His gaze flicked to the sigil, then to the deck still warm against my side. "But the Fold does not accept generosity, Sacrificiant. It accepts *truth*." The lanterns swayed again, though the air was still. Their flames bent toward something in the dark, something that hadn't been there before.

A figure emerged from the shadows—a silhouette too tall, too still, framed by the dying light like a man carved from the same silver as the sigil. Julian. His slate-blue skin caught the last of the lantern's glow, the silver Weave-Script across his collarbone pulsing once, twice, before settling into a slow, deliberate swirl. His long black hair curtained his face, obscuring the scar at his wrist, the one that always caught the light when he was nervous. He didn't speak. He didn't need to. The air between us thickened, charged with something older than desire, older than recognition.

The sigil on my palm burned brighter, white-hot, and the threads of light began to unravel from my skin, drifting toward him like smoke drawn to flame. The deck grew heavier in my grip, the edges biting into my fingers. "You feel it too," he said, and his voice was rough, like stone dragged across stone. "The pull. The cost." He stepped closer, the floorboards groaning under his weight, and the shadows at his feet didn't follow him. They shrank away.

Chayr's hand lifted from the table, fingers curling into a fist. "Julian." My name wasn't a greeting. It was a warning.

Julian didn't look at him. His eyes—dark, dark green in this light—were fixed on me, on the sigil, on the threads of light that had begun to weave themselves into the air between us. "The sigil is yours," he said. "But the cost isn't just a memory." He held out his hand, palm up, and the silver threads of the sigil stretched toward him, obedient as a dog to its master. "It's the memory of *who you were* when you loved him. And the knowledge of who you became when you lost him."

The lanterns flickered in unison, their light pooling in the hollow of his throat. The sigil's threads touched his skin—and the world held its breath.

I knew, without being told, that if I let him take it, I would be giving him something I'd never meant to give. Something that had shaped me in ways I wasn't ready to admit.

The deck was warm in my hand. It was always warm.

And the choice sat in the space between his outstretched hand and my own refusal to move.

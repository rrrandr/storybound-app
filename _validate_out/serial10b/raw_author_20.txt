The heavy oak table groaned under the weight of scrolls, their edges frayed and curling like the edges of a forgotten dream. The silver light from the sigil on my palm skittered across the grain, thin ribbons of it catching the dull sheen of the parchment—each flicker illuminating a name I had not yet spoken aloud. The deck pressed against my ribs, its edges biting into skin as if urging caution. I kept my palm flat against the wood, though the heat of the sigil had long since bled into the planks. The charred edge of my skin sent up a thin curl of smoke that smelled of old iron and something like crushed herbs.

The air thickened. Not with smoke, but with the scent of too many bodies pressed close, their bare soles scraping against stone now slick with sweat and old wax. The assembly had edged forward again, their murmurs a low tide that threatened to drown the room. I felt their breath on my neck before I saw them—warm and damp, like the exhalations of a beast that had been holding its tongue too long. The Dohkar’s band-muffled mouth moved, but no sound escaped. His silence was a blade unsheathed in the dark, and I refused to flinch.

Then it happened.

A shadow shifted. Not from the crowd—no, they were still as statues, their Weave-Script flickering in muted hues of aqua and gold and violet, all of them angled toward the tapestry-draped alcove as if a god had descended. The shadow came from *behind*. A figure, taller than most, leaned into the half-light where the lanterns failed to reach. The silhouette of a man, broad-shouldered and unnaturally still, his long dark hair framing a face that could have been carved from slate.

Julian.

I knew it was him before his hand turned the ring on his finger—just once, a slow rotation of thumb and forefinger that caught the dim light and sent a spark skittering across the floorboards. The assembly’s breath hitched. Not in fear. In recognition.

“You’ll speak when *I* allow it,” I said, my voice low and even, the words dragged through teeth that wanted to clench. The sigil’s glow dimmed—not from the pressure of my palm, but from the force of my will. It resisted, of course. The Fold *always* resisted. But it yielded, just a fraction. Enough.

The shadow leaned closer. The scent of crushed spiralgrass and iron filled the space between us. “I’ve never needed your permission, little Sacrificiant,” he murmured, though his lips did not move in time with the words. The voice came from everywhere and nowhere—carried on the breath of the Thirteen, perhaps, or the sigh of the Hungry Eye. “But I’ll take what you owe me.”

The scroll at my knees curled inward, its edges blackening as if burned from within. The name written there—*my* name—began to smudge, the ink bleeding into the parchment like a wound reopening. The assembly drew back as one, their Weave-Script flaring white-hot in waves of panic. The Dohkar’s hands, planted flat on the table, trembled. Not from fear. From the weight of an old debt coming due.

I did not look away. “Then take it,” I said. “But you’ll have to ask me first.” My fingers twitched toward the scroll, not to save it, but to ensure it would be legible when the moment came to name what I had sacrificed.

The shadow stepped into the light.

And the room held its breath.

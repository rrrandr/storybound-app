The lanterns flickered again, their uneven glow sliding across the scarred oak table like a slow, hesitating breath. The sigil on my palm pulsed, thin silver threads skittering across the grain of the wood as if the floor itself remembered what had been wished into being. The name on the scroll at my knees still curled away from the light, its ink bleeding into the parchment like old blood. I kept my palm flat against the table, the sigil’s heat bleeding into the planks, anchoring me to the moment. The assembly’s whispers thickened around the edges of the room, bare feet shifting on the cracked boards, yet no one turned to me directly. The Dohkar’s band-muffled mouth moved, but no sound escaped—only the faintest rustle of cloth against skin.

I ran my thumb along the edge of my sleeve, the old habit of twisting the fabric around my finger a useless comfort against the heat creeping up my wrist. The lanterns dipped, and for a heartbeat the shadows pooled too thickly around my knees, obscuring the scroll. Then the light steadied, and I saw it: a second scroll, tucked half beneath the one I already held, its edge peeking out like a secret waiting to be noticed. My pulse stuttered. I hadn’t placed it there. The sigil’s light bent toward it, silver fingers tracing the parchment’s curve as if it were something precious—or dangerous.

A memory flickered behind my eyes, sharp and unwanted: Julian standing too still on the fringes of the gathering, his long dark hair catching the dim light, the scar at the corner of his left eye holding the glow like a blade catching fire. The memory wasn’t mine. It was his—the way he’d looked at me when I’d first offered my palm to the Dohkar, the way his gaze had lingered on the sigil like it held an answer he already dreaded. I swallowed, the movement catching the nearest whisper in its wake. The assembly shifted again, a collective exhalation that made the lanterns gutter.

The scroll wasn’t mine. I knew that before I even touched it. The ink was too fresh, the parchment too smooth, the way it lay across the others too deliberate. My fingers trembled as I lifted it, the sigil’s light clinging to the edges like it wanted to read what was written before I did. The words resolved slowly in the dim light: *To the one who forgets what she owes.* The letters were bold, confident, the kind of handwriting I’d seen only once—on a letter Julian had burned in front of me, his mouth set in that way that meant he’d decided something irreversible.

The lanterns flickered again, and this time the silver threads from the sigil on my palm snagged on the new scroll’s edge, pulling the parchment just enough to reveal one more line, scrawled in the same hand: *You were never meant to remember.*

A floorboard popped under someone’s heel at the back of the room. The sigil’s glow steadied, but the air between the table and the door had gone too thin, too sharp. I should have spoken. I should have demanded answers. Instead, the scroll’s words settled into my ribs like a blade, and for the first time, I understood what the sigil had been trying to tell me all along: the sacrifice wasn’t just memory. It was forgetting. And Julian had already decided what I would forget.

The whispers thickened, the shadows leaning in. The choice was already made for me. The only question left was whether I would read the name aloud before the Dohkar found the will to silence the room again—or whether I would let the lanterns swallow the truth whole.

The threshold beneath my boot is carved with a sigil the color of old blood, its edges blurred as if licked by flame. I crouched lower, the gossamer of my tunic brushing the crimson spiralgrass that never grew this far from a First Sacrifice. The silver mark on my palm pulsed once, sharp as a needle pushed through skin, then settled into a slow, deep heat. I clenched my fingers until the brand stung, but the light leaked between them anyway. The assembly had already seen it. The Dohkar’s gaze, heavy as a held breath, had settled on the sigil the moment it bloomed. No one spoke the word that had bent. They did not need to.

My great-grandmother’s deck lay warm against my ribs, its gold edges catching what little light slipped through the veil-canopy overhead. One card had already slipped free — Petition Fate etched in heavy letters across the bottom. I hadn’t drawn it. The deck had. The weight of that truth sat in my gut like a swallowed stone.

The older woman who had spoken earlier stood at the edge of the clearing, her silver Weave-Script flickering across her throat in slow stutters of amethyst. “You asked Fate for a fool,” she said again, softer now, as if she’d heard herself the first time and still didn’t believe it. The spiralgrass at Seren’s knees darkened, a single patch turning the color of old blood while the rest stayed bright. Her eyes stayed on me, but the recognition that had always lived behind them was gone. She had paid what she named. I wanted to reach for her, to shake the memory back in, but my hand wouldn’t obey. It hovered over the sigil like it belonged to someone else.

A shadow detached itself from the edge of the clearing. Not the Dohkar. Not Seren. A shape too still, too deliberate. My fingers twitched near the sigil — the old ache of wanting to understand what I’d done, what it had cost. The sigil burned brighter, and for a heartbeat the world tilted. The threshold beneath me wasn’t just a line of carved stone anymore. It was a seam. A place where the Ashen Verge could unravel just enough to let something through.

The older woman’s hand lifted, her palm glowing with the same silver mark I wore. “You’re standing on the edge of something that remembers your name,” she said. “The Fold doesn’t forget. Neither should you.”

I exhaled through my nose, the torn band around my mouth fluttering once before I let it fall. The cloth’s whisper was the only sound in the clearing. The sigil’s heat climbed my wrist, then my arm, pooling behind my ribs like molten lead. I knew what it meant to stand here. To step over. To pay what Fate had already named. Julian’s face rose in my mind — the way he went still right before he laughed, like he was checking that it was allowed.

The older woman stepped closer. “The Verge doesn’t wait for the willing. It takes what it’s owed.”

A gust of wind cut through the clearing, carrying the scent of spiralgrass and something older — the mineral tang of the Fold itself. The shadows at the threshold deepened, then shifted. The sigil on my palm flared, and for a heartbeat I saw the outline of a door that hadn’t been there before. A door that wanted to open.

My breath hitched. The deck beneath my ribs pulsed once, as if in answer. I realized I was leaning forward — the slight lean of someone pulled by an invisible thread to the person she desired, even when that person wasn’t in the room. The sigil’s edges blurred at the corners, then sharpened again. It was waiting. I was hesitating.

“Choose,” the older woman said. “Or the choice will be made for you.”

The deck warmed against my side. One of the heavy gold cards slipped out — Tempt Fate is inscribed on it.

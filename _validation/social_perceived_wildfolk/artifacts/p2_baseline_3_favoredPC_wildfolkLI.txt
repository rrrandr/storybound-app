[TITLE: "The Weight of Assumptions"]
[SYNOPSIS: At a Thornwild waystation, a First Favored woman and her Wildfolk companion confront a traveller's public slight, where silence risks complicity and speech risks exposing the fragile bonds that hold identity together in a land shaped by sacrifice.]

The traveller's voice cut across the common room like a blade on stone.

"Look at him. Wildfolk or no, he's still got that look—like something the Field half-finished and left to rot."

I felt the words land before I turned. Thorne's hand, resting on the scarred bench between us, went still. The fire popped in the hearth, sending a thin thread of smoke toward the low beams. A few travellers shifted on their benches, eyes flicking away. The locals—two Keepers with thorn-cloaked shoulders, a woman nursing a cup of blush—went quiet in that particular way that meant they had heard this before.

Garrick leaned forward, elbows on the table, smiling as if he had offered a compliment. "No offense meant. Just saying what everyone sees. The Becoming takes what it wants, and the rest of us pay the price of looking."

Thorne's voice came low, even. "You see what you came to see."

The room held its breath. I could feel the Weave-Script under my own skin stirring, slow tidal flows of light that betrayed the heat rising in my chest. I wanted to speak—to cut the assumption at its root—but the words tangled with the memory of every other outsider who had measured Thorne the same way and found him wanting.

Is this a threat to our standing — or a challenge to what we share?

Garrick laughed once, short and sharp. "See? Even he knows."

Thorne stood. The motion was deliberate, the way Wildfolk moved when they refused to give the Field more than it had already taken. He did not raise his voice. "You speak of prices you have never paid."

The blush-brewer behind the counter set a cup down harder than necessary. One of the Keepers turned his face toward the door as if the night outside might offer escape. I rose with Thorne, the bench scraping against the packed earth floor. The choice pressed at my ribs: let the slight pass and keep the fragile peace of this waystation, or answer and risk drawing every eye to the truth of what Thorne and I were to each other.

My hand tightened around my great-grandmother's old tarot deck. I didn't remember bringing it. I toyed with the idea of letting the cards decide.
